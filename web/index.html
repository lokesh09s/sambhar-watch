How radar sees water

NISAR sends microwave pulses down at an angle and records how much comes back. Calm water acts like a mirror: it bounces the pulse away from the satellite, so water looks dark. Land, plants and buildings scatter some of the pulse back, so they look brighter.

We call a pixel “water” when its HH backscatter is darker than a fixed threshold. Radar works through cloud and at night, which matters at Sambhar because the lake changes most during the monsoon, when optical satellites are often blind.

The water-detection frequency of a spot is the number of dates it was detected as water divided by the number of dates with a valid radar observation there.

Limitations

  • The NISAR data are provisional: calibrated but only partly validated by the mission.
  • Wet salt crust can look as dark as water to radar. This is our main source of error, and we test it separately against Sentinel-2.
  • Radar dates are 12 days apart at best, with some longer gaps. The maps show water on those dates, not continuous presence.
  • Radar tells us where the surface is wet, not how deep the water is.
  • Salt-pan boundaries were drawn by hand. “Outside mapped pans” may still include unmapped pans, channels or roads.
  • These are surface observations only. They say nothing about whether any salt pan is legal.
