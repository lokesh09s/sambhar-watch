The problem

A lake that is hard to watch

Sambhar Salt Lake, about 70 km west of Jaipur, is a Ramsar wetland of 24,000 hectares. Salt works operate inside it. It changes most during the monsoon, when clouds hide it from optical satellites, and its salt crust can look like water.

How it works

How radar sees water

1

Brightness

Calm water reflects the radar pulse away, so it looks dark. But smooth salt crust is dark too.

2

Coherence

Compare two passes 12 days apart. Water changes; dry crust and soil stay the same.

3

Stress test

Repeat with 12 threshold settings, and show the range as an uncertainty band.

4

Check

Compare with 203 points labelled by eye on Sentinel-2 images (177 inside the lake).

NISAR sends microwave pulses down at an angle and records how much comes back. Calm water acts like a mirror: it bounces the pulse away from the satellite, so water looks dark. Land, plants and buildings scatter some of the pulse back, so they look brighter.

Radar works through cloud and at night, which matters at Sambhar because the lake changes most during the monsoon, when optical satellites are often blind.

Darkness alone is not enough: smooth dry salt crust can look as dark as water. So we also use coherence: how much the ground stayed the same between two passes 12 days apart. Water never stays the same, so its coherence is low. Dry crust, soil and buildings stay the same, so theirs is high.

Our provisional rule: a pixel is a dark unstable surface when it is dark in HH and its coherence is low. We call this “water or wet brine”, not simply water, until Sentinel-2 images confirm it. Each date is compared with the pass 12 days later (or 12 days earlier when there is no later one); dates with neither are left out.

The detection frequency of a spot is the number of dates it was detected as a dark unstable surface divided by the number of dates with a valid radar observation there.

Results

What we found

Sambhar dries out after the monsoon, and NISAR radar sees it, but only with coherence. Provisional

  • Brightness alone misses the change. Between 15 August and 14 October, radar brightness alone barely changes (about 125 to 115 km² inside the lake outline), because dry, smooth salt crust is as dark as water.
  • Coherence finds it. Crust stays the same between passes 12 days apart; water doesn't. With coherence, the detected area falls from about 91 to about 56 km² (threshold ranges 51–110 and 34–72).
  • The drop holds under every threshold setting we tested. In all 12 settings, the detected area falls from 15 August to 14 October, by about 17–39 km². Quad-pol dates in September and October agree (56–60 km²).
  • Sentinel-2 confirms the drying. The basin goes from about 55–60% water in late August to about 55–60% white crust by 13 October; 10 October looks the same.
  • Validation shows a trade-off. 203 points were labelled blind on Sentinel-2 images (11 Jan, 11 May, 29 Aug); 177 of them are inside the lake and are water or non-water (table below). 76% of these were water, so plain accuracy rewards over-counting: a model that always says “water” scores 76%. Weighting water and non-water equally, coherence is better (68% vs 58%), while brightness still finds more water. Brightness alone calls 81% of salt-crust points water; with coherence, 24%. Which is closer depends on the lake's state: coherence agrees better when crust spreads after the monsoon (late August, 73% vs 62%), brightness when the lake is full of calm winter water (January, 86% vs 44%). The drying season, where our headline sits, is exactly where coherence validated better.
  • Together they bracket the truth. Brightness alone gives a rough upper bound and coherence a rough lower bound; the chart shows both.
  • The 11 May optical trap. A single optical image can mislead: Sentinel-2 shows a white lake on 11 May but water on 6 and 16 May. Radar with coherence called 17 of the 19 white points water.

Balanced accuracy is the average of “water found” and “non-water found”, so it is not inflated by the many water points. An MNDWI audit flagged 5 of 203 blind labels as mixed-edge or white-film pixels; all labels were kept unchanged to avoid post-hoc bias.

Honest limits. Radar still overestimates in October (56 km² against about 30–40 km² of water seen in Sentinel-2, likely the reservoir and wet edges). Changes between April and August are within the uncertainty. Only April to October forms one consistent radar series. Radar misses a lot of winter water: in January (quad-pol mode) it detects about 50–55% of the lake against about 85–90% water in Sentinel-2. Why is unresolved. Possible causes: calm, stable shallow water or thin brine films; stable features smaller than a pixel (salt rafts, mud ridges); or coherence estimates biased upward where the radar signal is weak.

What this means Provisional

  • Use brightness and coherence together. Brightness over-counts salt crust; coherence under-counts water. Report both as upper and lower bounds; which is closer depends on the lake's state (coherence after the monsoon, brightness when the lake is full).
  • Always show the uncertainty. Small changes can come from threshold choices, not from the lake.
  • Don't mix NISAR radar modes for brightness without checking. Coherence is more consistent between modes.
  • In the dry season, use radar and optical together. Optical images can show a white film on water; the two together are safer than either alone.

Hypothesis, not proven

Why does the lake change?

Monsoon

Roughly July to September: rain and seasonal streams flow into the shallow lake.

After the rains

Strong evaporation shrinks the water, leaving wet brine and then salt crust.

Salt works

Brine is pumped into the mapped pans to evaporate, so the pans follow a managed cycle, not only the weather.

What we did not see: no clear monsoon rise in radar water area. The per-date uncertainty (40–72 km²) is larger than the April–August changes, and shallow water is hard to separate from wet crust.

Which polarization?

  • We used HH backscatter plus 12-day coherence. Coherence is not a polarization: it measures how stable the surface is between two passes.
  • We did not test HV. This is a reason, not a result: over the lake HV is likely close to the noise floor (about −26.8 dB), and HH usually gives better water/land contrast at L-band.
  • Mode check we did do: quad-pol (QPDH) HH is about 0.9 dB brighter than dual-pol (DHDH); coherence agrees between the modes.

Honest limits

Limitations

  • The NISAR data are provisional: calibrated but only partly validated by the mission.
  • Wet salt crust and brine are dark and unstable too, so they are counted with water. Dry crust that is dark but stable is removed by the coherence check. Both are tested separately against Sentinel-2.
  • Coherence needs a second pass 12 days before or after, so dates without one are left out.
  • The areas depend on the chosen thresholds. The shaded band on the chart shows by how much; differences between dates smaller than the band are not reliable.
  • Two radar modes alternate (dual-pol DHDH and quad-pol QPDH). On stable ground QPDH reads about 0.9 dB brighter in HH, so the main series uses only the 12 DHDH dates (April to October); QPDH dates are kept separate. Two DHDH dates (15 Aug, 14 Oct) take their coherence from a pair that mixes the modes.
  • Radar dates are 12 days apart at best, with some longer gaps. The maps show water on those dates, not continuous presence.
  • Radar tells us where the surface is wet, not how deep the water is.
  • Salt-pan boundaries were drawn by hand. “Outside mapped pans” may still include unmapped pans, channels or roads.
  • These are surface observations only. They say nothing about whether any salt pan is legal.
  • The lake outline was hand-drawn from optical imagery and misses part of the western lake bed (10.7 km², visible in the radar). Reported areas are inside the outline only, so they understate the full lake bed. Including it does not change the August to October drop: it holds in all 12 threshold settings.
  • Processing shortcuts: radar images are resampled to 20 m by nearest neighbour (no multilooking); the 3×3 smoothing is done in decibels, not in linear power; and GUNW coherence is estimated over a coarser window than the 10 m GCOV backscatter.

Strength: every date comes from the same orbit track and viewing geometry, so the radar's incidence angle is constant through the year.
